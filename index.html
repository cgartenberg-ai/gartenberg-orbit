147704 GARTENBERG

A small body.
A personal orbit.

This asteroid is named after Claudine Gartenberg. Its naming citation recognizes her high-school measurements of asteroid Gaspra’s light curve, before the Galileo spacecraft’s 1991 flyby.

Today, she studies organizations and institutions. This is a small detour back to physics.

Real orbits, an illustrated universe.

Planetary and asteroid positions begin with heliocentric J2000 orbital elements from NASA/JPL Horizons, all at the same epoch: September 9, 2026. Kepler’s equation advances every body on one shared clock, preserving each orbit’s eccentricity, inclination and orientation.

This is a two-body orbital illustration, not a precision ephemeris. The model holds the orbital elements fixed; long-term gravitational perturbations are not included. Distances are to scale. Bodies, the Sun, and Gartenberg’s marker are enlarged to remain visible. The background stars are decorative; the orbit paths carry the data.

Earth’s rotation uses its sidereal day (23 h 56 m). The other planets rotate at their relative sidereal rates. Rotational phases are illustrative, not a prediction of which longitude faces the Sun. Gartenberg’s close-up is an artist’s impression: an irregular rocky body on a slow turntable. Its shape, surface, spin rate and axial tilt are illustrative; no measured shape or pole model was found in the catalogs checked. The turntable is independent of the simulation clock. Its position in the solar system remains a marker on the measured orbit.

The default playback advances one Earth orbit every three seconds. Choose a slower preset to linger over the orbital dance.
